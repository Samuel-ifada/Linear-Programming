\section{Introduction}
The discovery of new structural materials for fusion energy systems requires evaluating large composition spaces against multiple performance constraints. Operating environments inside next-generation fusion reactors impose simultaneous requirements on the structural alloy: high refractoriness to withstand first-wall and divertor temperatures, irradiation tolerance under intense fast-neutron flux, sufficient ductility and fracture toughness for engineering use, manufacturability at reactor scale, and a minimal long-term radiological footprint to avoid handling and disposal hazards~\cite{zinkle2000operating,bloom2007critical}. Each of these requirements can be expressed as a threshold on a calculable quantity, and an alloy qualifies for further study only if every threshold is satisfied at once. Reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic steels such as EUROFER97~\cite{lindau2005eurofer} and emerging refractory high-entropy alloys~\cite{ref4,ref5} occupy distinct regions within this constraint-bounded design space.
The conventional screening workflow generates every candidate composition on a discrete grid using simplex enumeration tools such as nimplex~\cite{krajewski2024efficient}, evaluates a property or response model on each, and filters the result against the imposed thresholds. For an eleven-element system at 5~at.\% resolution, the grid contains approximately $3 \times 10^{7}$ compositions; finer resolutions or larger element sets push the count into the hundreds of millions. The computational cost of this generate-and-filter workflow scales linearly with the full grid size, even when only a small fraction of candidates ultimately satisfy the screening criteria.
Among these constraints, the neutron-activation response is the most computationally demanding to evaluate. Activation responses depend on the energy spectrum of incident neutrons, the irradiation time, and the decay time at which the response is assessed; computing them requires tracking dozens of unstable isotopes through transmutation chains at every grid point. Full inventory codes such as FISPACT-II~\cite{ref6} are prohibitive on a fine multicomponent grid, motivating the development of reduced-order models such as the FLARE framework~\cite{flare}, which exploits the additive structure of activation responses across pure-element contributions to evaluate alloy responses as a linear combination of pre-tabulated per-element quantities. FLARE-based filtering is typically performed against thresholds expressed as a small multiple of the response of a reference alloy such as pure tungsten or EUROFER97~\cite{ref3}.

Even with reduced-order models, the per-composition evaluation step remains the dominant cost when screening millions of compositions, and existing alloy-screening pipelines for fusion structural materials~\cite{ref4,ref5,ref9,ref10} preserve this generate-and-filter structure regardless of the response model used. The pipeline conventionally proceeds in sequence: composition enumeration, activation screening, thermodynamic equilibrium screening, and mechanical-property screening, with each step pruning the candidate set further. The activation step nonetheless evaluates the response on the full input set, by definition.

In this work, we introduce a constraint-first screening framework based on linear programming (LP) that eliminates the per-composition response evaluation step entirely whenever the underlying response model is linear in alloy composition. The key observation is that when a response model is linear, the set of compositions satisfying any threshold-based screening criterion forms a convex polytope on the atomic-fraction simplex: a region in composition space whose boundary is defined by the model coefficients and threshold values, not by the individual evaluation of candidate compositions. Linear-in-composition response models are common in materials design; rule-of-mixtures property estimators, effective-medium descriptors, additive composition--property models in catalysis and electronics, and the reduced-order activation framework FLARE~\cite{flare} all share this structure. Determining feasibility on such a model reduces to constructing the polytope from the linear coefficients, then enumerating its grid points by LP-guided recursion, a procedure that has well-developed tools in computational geometry and operations research.

We demonstrate the LP framework using the FLARE activation model on an eleven-element refractory alloy system spanning Ti-V-Ta-Nb-Mo-Zr-Cr-Hf-Fe-Re-W. The LP-defined survivor set reproduces the conventional FLARE-then-filter output bit-exactly while replacing the per-composition response evaluation step with a single polytope construction. We further demonstrate that the surviving compositions feed directly into the standard downstream thermodynamic and mechanical-property filters, providing a drop-in replacement for the activation screening step in conventional pipelines. The LP framework is general: it applies to any response model that is linear in alloy composition and offers an analytical alternative to brute-force generate-and-filter screening in this regime.
