\section{Methods}
\subsection{Linear Programming Framework}
Linear programming (LP) provides a mathematical framework for solving
optimization and feasibility problems subject to linear constraints. In the
present work, LP is used to reformulate activation screening as a feasibility
problem in composition space. Rather than generating candidate alloy compositions
and subsequently evaluating whether they satisfy prescribed activation criteria,
the activation criteria are expressed directly as constraints on alloy
composition. The set of compositions satisfying all constraints defines a
feasible region within the atomic-fraction simplex. This reformulation shifts the
focus from evaluating individual compositions to constructing and exploring the
admissible region of composition space itself.

The applicability of LP to the present problem arises from the linear dependence
of FLARE activation responses on alloy composition. As a result, the activation
criteria can be expressed as a system of linear inequalities whose solution set
forms a convex polytope. The geometry of this feasible space can be characterized
analytically through vertex enumeration, while feasible alloy compositions can be
obtained through LP-guided exploration of the polytope. The framework therefore
replaces conventional generate-and-filter screening with a constraint-first
methodology that directly identifies compositions satisfying the prescribed
activation requirements.

\subsubsection{Alloy design domain and FLARE response model}

The alloy design domain considered in this work consists of the eleven-element
refractory system comprising Ti, V, Ta, Nb, Mo, Zr, Cr, Hf, Fe, Re, and W. An
alloy is represented by its atomic-fraction vector
\(\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\), with \(N=11\), where \(x_i\) is the atomic fraction of
element \(i\). Physically admissible compositions lie on the standard
\((N-1)\)simplex:


\vspace{1em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
=
\Big\{
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:
\sum_i x_i=1,\;
x_i\ge0
\Big\}
}
\label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}

\vspace{1em}


The discrete design domain is the regular 5~at.\% grid on \(\Delta\), i.e.,
the set of compositions whose fractions are integer multiples of 0.05. On an
eleven-element simplex, this grid contains approximately \(3\times10^7\)
compositions, illustrating the computational burden associated with conventional
generate-and-filter screening.

Candidate alloys are screened based on their neutron-activation response using the
FLARE reduced-order model. FLARE computes each activation response of an alloy as
a composition-weighted sum of pre-tabulated pure-element contributions, so that
every response is linear in alloy composition. Responses are evaluated at a
neutron flux of \(8.0\times10^{13}~\mathrm{n~cm^{-2}~s^{-1}}\), an irradiation time of 2 years, and the
decay times associated with each screening criterion.

Screening is performed using five activation responses: gas production
\(M_{\mathrm{GAS}}\), total heat output \(HT_T\), and displacement damage
\(DPA\) at a decay time of approximately 1~s, together with contact dose rate
\(DOSE\) at decay times of 1~month and 100~years. Each response is assembled from its
per-element contributions on the appropriate extensive basis by weight
fraction for the mass-extensive responses \((M_{\mathrm{GAS}}, HT_T,\) and
\(DOSE)\) and by atomic fraction for the atom-extensive response \((DPA)\). This
distinction determines the exact form of the linear constraints constructed in
Section~2.1.2. Activation thresholds are defined relative to pure W and EUROFER97
reference response vectors, as described in the LP formulation in the following
section.

\subsubsection{LP Constraint Construction}

The activation screening criteria are imposed directly on the FLARE response model
and reformulated as linear inequalities in composition space. Because FLARE
responses are linear in alloy composition, each screening criterion can be
expressed as a constraint on the atomic-fraction vector \(x\). The linear response
coefficients are collected in the response matrix

\vspace{1em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times N},\quad
A_{r,i}=f_r(e_i)
}
\label{eq:activation_matrix}
\end{equation}

\vspace{1em}


where \(A_{r,i}\) denotes response \(r\) of pure element \(i\), with \(R=5\)
screening responses and \(N=11\) elements. Each screening threshold is defined
relative to a reference alloy,

\vspace{1em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
\tau_r=\phi\, b_r,\;
\phi=10
}
\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}

\vspace{1em}


where \(\mathbf{b}=(b_1,\ldots,b_R)\) is the reference response vector, taken as either pure
tungsten \((b_W)\) or EUROFER97 \((b_E)\), and \(\phi\) is the threshold
multiplier.

Each criterion is then expressed as a linear inequality in the atomic-fraction
vector \(\mathbf{x}\). The distinction in extensive basis introduced in Section~2.1.1
determines its form. The atom-extensive response \(DPA\) combines directly by atomic
fraction and is therefore already linear in \(\mathbf{x}\),

\vspace{1em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
\sum_i x_i A_{r,i}
\le
\phi b_r
}
\label{eq:atom_constraint}
\end{equation}

\vspace{1em}

The mass extensive responses \((M_{\mathrm{GAS}}, HT_T,\) and \(DOSE)\) combine
by weight fraction, defined as

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
w_i
=
\frac{x_i M_i}
{\sum_j x_j M_j}
}
\label{eq:weight_fraction}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5em}


where \(M_i\) is the atomic mass of element \(i\). Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:weight_fraction}
into the natural constraint \(\sum_i w_i A_{r,i}\le \phi b_r\) introduces the
composition-dependent denominator \(\sum_j x_j M_j\), making the expression nonlinear in
\(\mathbf{x}\). Because this denominator is strictly positive on the simplex, multiplying
through by it preserves the inequality while eliminating the nonlinearity, yielding
the equivalent linear form

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
\sum_i x_i M_i \left(A_{r,i}-\phi b_r\right)
\le 0
}
\label{eq:mass_constraint}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5em}


Equations~\eqref{eq:atom_constraint} and~\eqref{eq:mass_constraint} are both linear in
\(\mathbf{x}\). Stacking all \(R\) screening criteria together with the box bounds
\(0\le x_i\le1\) yields the linear inequality system

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\le \mathbf{h}
}
\label{eq:linear_system}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5em}


in which the \(DPA\) row contributes the coefficients \(A_{r,i}\) with right-hand side
\(\phi b_r\), while each mass-extensive response contributes the transformed
coefficients \(M_i(A_{r,i}-\phi b_r)\) with right-hand side zero. Together with the
simplex equality constraint
\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
\sum_i x_i = 1
}
\label{eq:simplex_equality}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5em}

The resulting system defines the complete LP feasibility problem. The solution set
of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:linear_system}--\eqref{eq:simplex_equality} forms the feasible
activation region characterized in the following section.

\subsubsection{Feasible Polytope and Vertex Enumeration}
The inequality system of Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear_system}, combined with the simplex
equality constraint of Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex_equality}, defines the set of all
compositions satisfying every screening criterion as the convex polytope

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
P=
\left\{
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta :
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf{h}
\right\}
}
\label{eq:polytope}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5em}


The geometry of \(P\) is determined entirely by the FLARE coefficients and the
chosen screening thresholds. Every composition lying within \(P\) satisfies the
activation screen, whereas every composition outside \(P\) fails at least one
criterion by construction. The polytope \(P\) therefore provides an analytical
description of the feasible design space, obtained without brute-force evaluation
of the discrete candidate-alloy space.

The feasible region is characterized geometrically through its vertices. A vertex
is a point at which the simplex equality holds together with \(N-1\) active
inequality constraints, so that \(N\) independent equalities determine a unique
point. For each combination of \(N-1\) active constraint rows, indexed by an active
set \(\mathcal{A}\), the corresponding vertex is obtained by solving the square
system

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{equation}
{\Huge
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{1}^{\top} \\
\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{A}}
\end{bmatrix}
}
\label{eq:vertex_system}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5em}

where \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\) enforces the simplex equality and
\(\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{A}}\) and \(\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{A}}\) are the rows selected by
\(\mathcal{A}\). Rank-deficient combinations are discarded. A candidate solution is
accepted as a vertex only if it remains feasible for the full system,
\(\mathbf{x}\in\Delta\) and \(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\le \mathbf{h}+\varepsilon\), with
numerical tolerance \(\varepsilon=10^{-9}\). Coordinates within \(\varepsilon\) of
0 or 1 are snapped to remove round-off. Deduplicating the accepted points yields
the exact vertex set of \(P\). The resulting vertex set provides an exact geometric
representation of the LP-defined feasible region and forms the basis for the
enumeration of feasible grid compositions described in the following section.

\subsubsection{Enumeration of Feasible Grid Compositions}

The compositions of interest are the alloys on the 5~at.\% grid that lie within
the feasible polytope \(P\). Rather than generating all grid compositions and
testing each for membership, the brute-force procedure, the framework is designed
to avoid; the feasible grid points are enumerated directly through a depth-first
recursion over the elements, with a linear program pruning the search at every
level.

At recursion depth \(p\), the fractions of elements \(1,\ldots,p-1\) are fixed, and
the remaining fractions \(x_{p:N}\) are free. Two linear programs over the free
variables determine the exact range of the next fraction \(x_p\) that still admits
a feasible completion of the alloy.

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x_p^{\min},\,x_p^{\max}
&=
\min/\max\; x_p \\
\text{s.t.}\quad
\mathbf{G}_{:,\,p:N}\mathbf{x}_{p:N}
&\le \mathbf{s}, \\
\sum_{k\ge p} x_k
&= \rho, \\
0 \le x_k
&\le \rho .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:recursive_lp}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5em}



This LP-based bound is required rather than a simpler monotonic test. Because the
transformed mass-extensive rows carry coefficients \(M_i(A_{r,i}-\phi b_r)\) whose
sign varies across elements, the accumulated constraint value is not monotone
along a partial composition; it may rise and fall as elements are added. A naive
pruning rule, such as terminating a branch once a running sum exceeds a bound,
would therefore be invalid. The program in Eq.~\eqref{eq:recursive_lp} instead
tests the feasibility of the entire remaining completion rather than the partial
sum alone, making the pruning exact and guaranteeing that the enumeration returns
every grid composition contained in \(P\) and no others.

The linear programs are solved using the HiGHS method through
\texttt{scipy.optimize.linprog}, and the feasible interval at each recursion level
is memoized to avoid re-solving recurring subproblems. FLARE responses are
computed for the enumerated compositions only after enumeration, solely for
reporting and never for screening. As shown in Section~3, the resulting survivor
set reproduces the conventional generate-and-filter output exactly.

\subsection{Analytical Mechanical Property Models}

Following activation screening, the remaining compositions were assessed using
analytical models of mechanical performance. Because direct experimental
characterization or high-fidelity mechanical simulations are impractical for the
large number of candidate alloys considered in this work, physically motivated
analytical models were employed as first-order estimates of strength and
ductility. These models provide a computationally efficient means of further
reducing the candidate composition space before the thermodynamic screening stage.

\subsubsection{Maresca-Curtin Model as First Approximation for Yield Strength}

The compositions surviving the LP-based activation screening stage were
subsequently evaluated using the Maresca-Curtin (MC) model as a first
approximation of alloy yield strength \cite{maresca2020mechanistic}. The MC model
has been successfully employed for the computational screening and design of
refractory multi-principal-element alloys \cite{vela2023data}, and the present
implementation follows the general methodology adopted in those studies. In this work, the model was used as an intermediate screening stage to identify
compositions possessing sufficient mechanical strength before thermodynamic
assessment.

The MC model is a physics-based framework that predicts the strength of
body-centered cubic (BCC) random solid solutions by relating the yield strength to the energetics of thermally activated dislocation motion. The temperature-dependent yield strength was calculated using the dislocation glide formulation proposed by
Maresca and Curtin.


\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tau_y(T,\dot{\varepsilon})
&= \tau_{y0}
\exp\!\left[
-\frac{1}{0.55}
\left(
\frac{kT}{\Delta E_b}
\ln\!\left(\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_0}{\dot{\varepsilon}}\right)
\right)^{0.91}
\right]
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:yield_stress}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5em}

where \(\tau_{y0}\) is the athermal yield strength, \(\Delta E_b\) is the activation
barrier for dislocation glide, \(T\) is the absolute temperature,
\(\dot{\varepsilon}\) is the applied strain rate, \(\dot{\varepsilon}_0\) is the
reference strain rate, and \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant. The corresponding tensile
yield strength was obtained through the appropriate Taylor-factor conversion.

Following the approach of Vela et al. \cite{vela2023data}, the physical inputs required by the model, including atomic volumes, elastic constants (\(C_{11}\),
\(C_{12}\), and \(C_{44}\)), shear moduli, and Poisson ratios, were assembled from
literature data and combined using the rule of mixtures to approximate the
effective properties of each alloy composition. Using these inputs, the model
evaluates the energetic barriers associated with dislocation motion and predicts the resulting temperature-dependent yield strength.


Yield strengths were evaluated at 25, 600, and \(650^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) using a
strain rate of \(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). The yield strength of each candidate
composition was calculated at these conditions and recorded as a screening
descriptor; no compositions were eliminated at this stage.

\subsection{Pugh Ratio as Indicator of Ductility}

In addition to strength, candidate alloys were evaluated using the Pugh ratio as a
first-order indicator of ductility. Proposed by Pugh, the ratio of bulk modulus to
shear modulus provides an empirical measure of the balance between resistance to
plastic deformation and resistance to fracture. Materials with larger \(B/G\)
values generally exhibit more ductile behavior, whereas smaller values are
associated with brittle fracture tendencies.

The Pugh ratio was calculated as

\begin{equation}
P = \frac{B}{G},
\end{equation}

where \(B\) is the bulk modulus and \(G\) is the shear modulus of the alloy. The
required elastic properties were obtained from composition-weighted estimates of
The constituent elemental properties using the rule of mixtures. For each
composition, the calculated Pugh ratio was compared against the prescribed
screening criterion to identify alloys possessing an acceptable balance of strength
and ductility.

The Pugh-ratio assessment complements the Maresca--Curtin strength predictions by
providing a simple analytical measure of ductility. As with the strength model, no
compositions were eliminated at this stage; the analytical strength and ductility
descriptors were computed for all LP-feasible compositions and used as screening
criteria in the subsequent down-selection.

\subsection{CALPHAD Thermodynamic Screening}

The alloy compositions identified by the LP framework were subsequently evaluated
using the CALPHAD (CALculation of PHAse Diagrams) method to characterize their
thermodynamic stability, phase constitution, melting behavior, and thermophysical
properties. CALPHAD has been widely employed for alloy design and phase-stability
prediction in complex concentrated and refractory alloy systems
\cite{li2023calphad,conway2022thermodynamic}. Thermodynamic calculations were performed in Thermo-Calc
using the TCHEA6 thermodynamic database \cite{TCHEA6}. For each composition, the
equilibrium state was obtained through Gibbs free-energy minimization over the
active elemental components.

Equilibrium phase calculations were performed at 25, 500, 600, 650, 700, 800, and
900 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), together with an elevated temperature of
2000 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). At each temperature, the stable phases and their
corresponding mole fractions were determined. These calculations were used to
characterize the evolution of phase constitution across the temperature range of
interest, including the formation of BCC solid-solution phases and competing
intermetallic phases such as sigma, Laves, mu, and chi.

The melting behavior of each alloy was evaluated using the Thermo-Calc Liquidus
and Solidus Temperature property model, which provides the liquidus and solidus
temperatures defining the thermodynamic processing window. Thermophysical
properties were subsequently evaluated using the Equilibrium with Freeze-in
Temperature property model, yielding density, heat capacity, thermal conductivity,
thermal resistivity, thermal diffusivity, electrical resistivity, electrical
conductivity, and thermal expansion. These properties were computed at both the
liquidus and solidus temperatures, as well as at fixed temperatures spanning
25--900 \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).

The resulting phase fraction, melting temperature, and thermophysical property data
were used to characterize the thermodynamic behavior of the candidate alloy
compositions and to provide the temperature-dependent inputs required for the
subsequent thermal analyses.

\subsubsection{ELM Melting Margin Modeling}

To quantify the resistance of candidate alloys to transient plasma loading, the ELM melting margin was evaluated as the temperature difference between the alloy melting temperature and the peak surface temperature reached during an ELM-like heat pulse. The surface temperature rise was computed using a one-dimensional, semi-infinite slab approximation, which is valid when the thermal penetration depth during the pulse is much smaller than the sample thickness. The model considers a background heat flux upon which a high-intensity pulse is superimposed for a short duration. The input parameters used in the heat balance formulation are listed in 
Table~\ref{tab:ELM_params}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Input parameters used to compute heat balance during ELM transient loading.}
\label{tab:ELM_params}
\begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{Xl}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\ 
\midrule
Temperature on the cooling side, $T_{\mathrm{bott}}$ & \SI{415}{\kelvin}~\cite{VandenKerkhof2021,DeTemmerman2018} \\
Steady-state power flux density, $Q_{\mathrm{SS}}$ & \SI{10}{\mega\watt\per\square\meter}~\cite{Pitts2019,VandenKerkhof2021} \\
ELM energy density, $E_{\mathrm{ELM}}$ & \SI{0.2}{\mega\joule\per\square\meter}~\cite{Farid2015} \\
Pulse duration, $t_{\mathrm{pulse}}$ & \SI{0.5}{\milli\second}~\cite{Farid2015,DeTemmerman2018} \\
First wall thickness, $d$ & \SI{6}{\milli\meter}~\cite{VandenKerkhof2021,DeTemmerman2018} \\ 
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The ELM heat flux was calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{ELM}} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{ELM}}}{t_{\mathrm{pulse}}}.
\end{equation}

Material thermal properties (thermal conductivity $k$, mass-specific heat capacity $c_p$, and density $\rho$) were obtained from CALPHAD calculations at multiple temperatures. For each alloy, the effective properties were computed as the arithmetic mean of values across the temperature range, with molar heat capacity converted to mass-specific heat capacity using the alloy molecular weight. Density was evaluated at room temperature.

The total surface temperature rise was calculated as the sum of steady-state and transient components. The steady-state temperature increase due to the continuous background heat flux was given by:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta T_{\mathrm{SS}} = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{SS}} \, d}{k},
\end{equation}
representing the linear temperature gradient through the first wall thickness.

The transient temperature rise caused by the ELM pulse was modeled as:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta T_{\mathrm{ELM}} = \frac{2 Q_{\mathrm{ELM}}}{k} 
    \sqrt{\frac{\alpha t_{\mathrm{pulse}}}{\pi}},
\end{equation}
where the thermal diffusivity is $\alpha = k/(\rho c_p)$.

The peak surface temperature was then obtained as:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\max} = T_{\mathrm{bott}} + \Delta T_{\mathrm{SS}} + \Delta T_{\mathrm{ELM}}.
\end{equation}

Finally, the ELM melting margin was defined as:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\mathrm{margin}} = T_m - T_{\max},
\end{equation}
\noindent where $T_m$ is the alloy solidus temperature.
