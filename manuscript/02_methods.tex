\section{Methods}
\subsection{Constraint-Based Neutronic Screening}
\label{sec:feasible-space}

The low-activation feasible space is defined over the eleven-element
\ch{Cr-Fe-Hf-Mo-Nb-Re-Ta-Ti-V-W-Zr} system, sampled on a \(5\)~at.\% grid, using
the \emph{FLARE} activation model~\cite{flare} together with linear programming.
\emph{FLARE} is a reduced-order model that estimates the neutron-activation
response of an alloy, gas generation, displacement damage, decay heat, and contact
dose rate, as a composition-weighted sum of precomputed elemental contributions,
providing a quantitative basis for assessing irradiation-induced phenomena across
the design space.

The responses are evaluated under the irradiation conditions summarized in
\Cref{tab:ind_variables}, a two-year irradiation time with decay times of one
second, one month, and 100 years, corresponding to the immediate post-irradiation,
maintenance, and long-term waste management scenarios. Under these conditions, the
responses of pure W and EUROFER97 serve as reference baselines for plasma-facing
and structural materials, respectively, and a composition is retained if each of
its responses stays within a deviation factor of \(\phi=10\) of the corresponding
baseline value.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Summary of irradiation condition inputs used in the \emph{FLARE} model to
evaluate activation responses for candidate alloys under first-wall neutron
environments.}
\label{tab:ind_variables}
\begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{lX}
\toprule
\multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Independent Variables}} \\
\midrule
First Wall Average Neutron Flux & \(8\times10^{13}~\mathrm{n/cm^2 s}\) \\
Irradiation Time               & 2 years \\
Decay Time                     & 1 second \& 1 month \& 100 years \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The conventional way to apply these baselines is to generate every composition,
evaluate its responses, and discard those that fall outside the limits. For a
\(5\)~at.\% grid this requires testing on the order of \(10^{7}\) compositions one
by one, which is slow and scales poorly as elements or resolution are added. We
avoid this entirely by using the structure of the screening problem itself, since
the FLARE responses are linear in the alloy composition
\(\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_N)\), directly for the atomic-fraction-averaged
displacement damage and, for the mass-weighted responses, once each criterion
is multiplied through by their common alloy-mass denominator. Linearity is what makes the shortcut
possible, since when a response varies linearly with composition, the boundary at which it crosses
a baseline limit is a flat surface in composition space, so each limit simply
divides the space into an allowed and a disallowed side rather than having to be
tested point by point. Each baseline limit is therefore a linear inequality in
\(\mathbf{x}\), and stacking all of them, with the simplex condition
\(\sum_i x_i = 1\) and the bounds \(0 \le x_i \le 1\), gives a single linear
system \(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{h}\). The compositions satisfying
every limit are exactly those lying inside the region enclosed by these surfaces, a
convex polytope

\begin{equation}
P=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta : \mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf{h}\}.
\label{eq:polytope}
\end{equation}

The feasible region is thus obtained directly as \(P\) by linear programming, and
the \(5\)~at.\% grid compositions inside it are enumerated without evaluating the
infeasible majority of the grid. This is substantially faster than
generate-and-filter and, importantly, it is not specific to FLARE, as the same
construction applies to any screening model whose responses are linear in
composition, FLARE being one such model. The resulting low-activation feasible
space is the starting point for the property evaluation and graded-path design that
follow.

\subsection{High-Throughput Property Calculations}
\label{sec:feasible-space-props}

Following activation screening, the surviving compositions were
characterized by high-throughput calculations of the properties on which the
design depends. Because direct experimental characterization is impractical
for the number of candidates considered in this work, physically motivated
analytical models were employed as first-order estimates, with the
Maresca-Curtin model providing yield strength and the Pugh ratio providing
intrinsic ductility. CALPHAD calculations then supplied the phase stability,
melting behavior, and thermophysical properties on which the property
screens and the classification of Section~\ref{sec:results} are built.


\subsubsection{Strength and Ductility Descriptors}
The feasible compositions were evaluated with the Maresca-Curtin (MC)
model as a first approximation of alloy yield strength
\cite{maresca2020mechanistic}, following the methodology previously applied
to the screening of refractory multi-principal-element alloys
\cite{vela2023data}. The model is a physics-based framework for
body-centered cubic random solid solutions that relates strength to the
energetics of thermally activated dislocation glide, with the
temperature-dependent yield strength given by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tau_y(T,\dot{\varepsilon})
&= \tau_{y0}
\exp\!\left[
-\frac{1}{0.55}
\left(
\frac{kT}{\Delta E_b}
\ln\!\left(\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_0}{\dot{\varepsilon}}\right)
\right)^{0.91}
\right]
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:yield_stress}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{y0}$ is the athermal yield strength, $\Delta E_b$ is the
activation barrier for dislocation glide, $T$ is the absolute temperature,
$\dot{\varepsilon}$ is the applied strain rate, $\dot{\varepsilon}_0$ is the
reference strain rate, and $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, with the tensile
yield strength obtained through the appropriate Taylor-factor conversion.

The physical inputs required by the model, including atomic volumes, elastic
constants ($C_{11}$, $C_{12}$, and $C_{44}$), shear moduli, and Poisson
ratios, were assembled from literature data and combined using the rule of
mixtures to approximate the effective properties of each composition. Yield
strengths were evaluated at 25, 600, and $650^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ at a strain
rate of $10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and recorded as mechanical-property
descriptors.

Intrinsic ductility was screened using the Pugh ratio \(B/G\), the ratio of
bulk to shear modulus, in the modern convention in which larger values
indicate greater ductility~\cite{pugh1954,singh2023ductility}; this is the
inverse of Pugh's original \(G/B\) formulation. For high-throughput
evaluation, the elemental Poisson ratios \(\nu_i\) were combined by the rule
of mixtures to give \(\bar{\nu}=\sum_i x_i \nu_i\), and the alloy Pugh ratio
was estimated through the isotropic relations \(E=3B(1-2\nu)\) and
\(G=E/2(1+\nu)\), under which the bulk modulus cancels and the descriptor
reduces to
\begin{equation}
\frac{B}{G}=\frac{2\,(1+\bar{\nu})}{3\,(1-2\bar{\nu})},
\label{eq:pugh}
\end{equation}
a function of the composition-averaged Poisson ratio alone. A composition was
classified as intrinsically ductile when \(B/G \ge 2.6\), a stricter cut than
the conventional \(B/G > 1.75\) criterion~\cite{singh2023ductility}. As a
rule-of-mixtures prior, this descriptor serves as a screening-level estimate;
first-principles or experimental validation of the selected compositions is
deferred to future work.


\subsubsection{CALPHAD Thermodynamic Calculations}
 
The feasible alloy compositions were evaluated using the CALPHAD
(CALculation of PHAse Diagrams) method to characterize their thermodynamic
stability, phase constitution, melting behavior, and thermophysical
properties. In the CALPHAD approach, the Gibbs free energy of every candidate
phase is described by parametric models whose coefficients are assessed from
experimental and first-principles data for the constituent binary and ternary
subsystems and extrapolated to higher-order systems, allowing equilibria in
multicomponent alloys to be computed where direct experimental phase data do
not exist. The method is widely employed for alloy design and phase-stability
prediction in complex concentrated and refractory alloy
systems~\cite{li2023calphad,conway2022thermodynamic}. Thermodynamic
calculations were performed in Thermo-Calc using the TCHEA6 thermodynamic
database, assessed specifically for high-entropy and refractory
multi-principal-element systems. For each composition, the equilibrium state
was obtained by global minimization of the total Gibbs free energy over the
active elemental components.

Equilibrium phase calculations were performed at 25, 500, 600, 650, 700, 800,
and 900~$^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$, together with an elevated temperature of
2000~$^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$, determining at each temperature the stable phases
and their mole fractions, including the BCC solid solution and competing
intermetallic phases such as sigma, Laves, mu, and chi. The melting behavior
of each alloy was evaluated using the Thermo-Calc Liquidus and Solidus
Temperature property model, and thermophysical properties, including density, heat
capacity, thermal conductivity and resistivity, thermal diffusivity,
electrical conductivity and resistivity, and thermal expansion, were obtained
with the Equilibrium with Freeze-in temperature property model at the liquidus
and solidus temperatures and at fixed temperatures spanning
25--900~$^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. The resulting phase-fraction, melting, and
thermophysical data provide the temperature-dependent inputs for the property
screens and the ELM melting-margin calculations that follow.

\subsection{Joinable Composition Regions}
\label{sec:joinable}

A graded transition requires a connected region of feasible composition
space. To identify such regions, the compositions surviving the property
filters were organized into a simplex-lattice graph, in which two
compositions are neighbors when they differ by a single grid step, one
element decreased and another increased by one increment~\cite{nimplex}.
Graph representations of the design space have likewise been used within
this framework for the design of compositionally graded
structures~\cite{multiterminalCGA2024}. Because the filters remove
compositions unevenly, this graph is generally not connected and separates
into connected components, regions within which any
two compositions are joined by a continuous chain of single-step feasible
neighbors. These components are the subgraphs, and the largest is carried
forward as the design region, since it offers the widest contiguous space
for a graded transition.

Within this largest subgraph, the graded alloy is designed as a
doubly-monotonic shortest path between two terminal compositions. Terminals
are restricted to deployable multi-component alloys, requiring at least
three constituents with no single element above \(90\)~at.\%, so that the
design is anchored by realistic alloys rather than the pure-element property
extrema. Within these pools, the plasma-facing terminal is the
maximum-solidus composition among the high-solidus candidates and the
structural terminal the maximum-Pugh composition among the ductile
candidates. Monotonicity of the transition is enforced as a hard constraint
on the graph itself. Each undirected lattice edge is expanded into its two directed
transitions, and a directed edge \(u\!\to\!v\) is retained only if it does
not increase the solidus and does not decrease the Pugh ratio,
\(T_s(v)\le T_s(u)\) and \(B/G(v)\ge B/G(u)\), with all other transitions
deleted. Dijkstra's algorithm with unit edge weights then returns the exact
shortest path on the pruned directed graph. Because monotonicity is imposed
by edge deletion rather than by a penalty term, the formulation contains no
tuning parameters, in contrast to cost-function approaches in which
monotonicity is promoted through a weight that must be
selected~\cite{kirk2021monotonic}. A penalized fallback exists for terminal
pairs disconnected by the strict pruning but was not required in this work.
The returned path is verified independently of its construction by per-step
assertions and by a loss-of-invariance metric, defined for each property as
the summed magnitude of all counter-trend increments along the path, which
is zero for a perfectly monotonic profile. Because each edge connects
compositions separated by a single grid step and the path is confined to the
property-acceptable subgraph, every intermediate composition of the graded
transition remains feasible by construction.



\subsection{ELM Melting Margin Modeling}
\label{subsec:elm-methods}

To quantify the resistance of candidate alloys to transient plasma loading, the ELM melting margin was evaluated as the temperature difference between the alloy melting temperature and the peak surface temperature reached during an ELM-like heat pulse. The surface temperature rise was computed using a one-dimensional, semi-infinite slab approximation, which is valid when the thermal penetration depth during the pulse is much smaller than the sample thickness. The model considers a background heat flux upon which a high-intensity pulse is superimposed for a short duration. The input parameters used in the heat balance formulation are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:ELM_params}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Input parameters used to compute heat balance during ELM transient loading.}
\label{tab:ELM_params}
\begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{Xl}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
\mbox{Temperature on the cooling side, $T_{\mathrm{bot}}$} & \SI{415}{\kelvin}~\cite{VandenKerkhof2021,DeTemmerman2018} \\
Steady-state power flux density, $Q_{\mathrm{SS}}$ & \SI{10}{\mega\watt\per\square\meter}~\cite{Pitts2019,VandenKerkhof2021} \\
ELM energy density, $E_{\mathrm{ELM}}$ & \SI{0.2}{\mega\joule\per\square\meter}~\cite{Farid2015} \\
Pulse duration, $t_{\mathrm{pulse}}$ & \SI{0.5}{\milli\second}~\cite{Farid2015,DeTemmerman2018} \\
First wall thickness, $d$ & \SI{6}{\milli\meter}~\cite{VandenKerkhof2021,DeTemmerman2018} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}


The ELM heat flux was calculated as

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{equation}
Q_{\mathrm{ELM}}
=
\frac{E_{\mathrm{ELM}}}{t_{\mathrm{pulse}}}.
\label{eq:elm_heat_flux}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.2em}

Material thermal properties, including thermal conductivity \(k\), mass-specific
heat capacity \(c_p\), and density \(\rho\), were obtained from CALPHAD
calculations at multiple temperatures. For each alloy, the effective properties
were computed as the arithmetic mean of values across the temperature range, with
molar heat capacity converted to mass-specific heat capacity using the alloy
molecular weight. Density was evaluated at room temperature.

The total surface temperature rise was calculated as the sum of steady-state and
transient components. The steady-state temperature increase due to the continuous
background heat flux was given by

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{equation}
\Delta T_{\mathrm{SS}}
=
\frac{Q_{\mathrm{SS}}\, d}{k},
\label{eq:ss_temp_rise}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.2em}

representing the linear temperature gradient through the first-wall thickness.

The transient temperature rise caused by the ELM pulse was modeled as

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{equation}
\Delta T_{\mathrm{ELM}}
=
\frac{2Q_{\mathrm{ELM}}}{k}
\sqrt{\frac{\alpha t_{\mathrm{pulse}}}{\pi}},
\label{eq:elm_temp_rise}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.2em}

where the thermal diffusivity is \(\alpha=k/(\rho c_p)\).

The peak surface temperature was then obtained as

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{equation}
T_{\max}
=
T_{\mathrm{bot}}
+
\Delta T_{\mathrm{SS}}
+
\Delta T_{\mathrm{ELM}}.
\label{eq:peak_temp}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.2em}

Finally, the ELM melting margin was defined as

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{margin}}
=
T_m
-
T_{\max},
\label{eq:melting_margin}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.2em}

\noindent where \(T_m\) is the alloy solidus temperature.
